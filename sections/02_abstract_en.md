Abstract

Autophagy is an important process that helps maintain cellular homeostasis and defend against viral infection. It may play a key role in antiviral immunity in the central nervous system. This study aimed to investigate how variants in the autophagy-related genes ATG4C and ATG4D affect human antiviral immunity. We first analyzed the distribution, population frequency, and predicted deleteriousness of ATG4C and ATG4D variants using large-scale population genetic databases. We then selected variants that were relatively common in the general population or were predicted to be highly deleterious, and overexpressed them in human cells to assess their effects on messenger RNA and protein expression. The results showed that predicted deleterious nonsynonymous variants in ATG4C and ATG4D were generally rare in the general population, suggesting that these two genes are under strong evolutionary constraint. In vitro expression experiments further showed that some predicted loss-of-function variants in ATG4C caused a loss of protein expression, possibly by affecting protein production or stability. In contrast, the tested ATG4D variants did not lead to obvious protein loss, although they may still affect protein function through mechanisms that were not assessed in this study. Related viral infection experiments also suggested that some deleterious ATG4C variants may increase cellular susceptibility to herpes simplex virus infection. In summary, this study found that rare deleterious biallelic variants in ATG4C and ATG4D are enriched in patients with severe viral infections of the central nervous system. These findings suggest that rare deleterious biallelic variants in ATG4 genes may cause a previously unrecognized form of autosomal recessive inborn error of immunity.

Keywords: autophagy; ATG4 family; ATG4C; ATG4D; antiviral immunity; central nervous system viral infection; genetic variants; inborn errors of immunity
