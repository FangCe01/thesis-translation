# 3 Results

## 3.1 Population Genetic Analysis of ATG4C and ATG4D

In the HGID laboratory database, we identified patients carrying biallelic variants — specifically, ATG4C Arg389Ter, ATG4D Ser89Asn, and ATG4D Arg382His — among a cohort of patients with herpes simplex virus encephalitis.

We retrieved genetic variant information for ATG4C and ATG4D from the gnomAD v4.1 database and filtered for nonsynonymous variants present in the homozygous state. Using CADD scores, allele frequencies, and the MSC95 threshold, we evaluated the distribution characteristics of nonsynonymous homozygous variants — particularly pLOF variants — in the general population and compared them with patient-derived variants (Fig. 3.1).

We found that nonsynonymous variants exceeding their respective MSC95 thresholds were relatively scarce, and that missense variants with high CADD scores had extremely low allele frequencies, indicating that ATG4C and ATG4D — especially ATG4D — are subject to strong purifying selection. This result is consistent with the important functions of ATG4C and ATG4D in autophagy, cellular homeostasis, and their broad expression across multiple tissues, suggesting that damaging variants in these genes are not tolerated in the general population.

Each point represents one or more nonsynonymous homozygous variants in the general population listed in gnomAD v4.1 for each homolog. At the same time, we observed that a small number of rare, high-impact nonsynonymous variants were still present in ATG4C and ATG4D, including three mutations in gnomAD and the HGID database that are associated with viral encephalitis patients. These mutations were extremely rare in the population and had CADD scores well above their MSC95 thresholds. This pattern does not contradict the purifying selection acting on ATG4C and ATG4D; rather, it suggests that against a background of strong constraint, a very small number of extremely rare, high-impact variants can persist at low frequency in the population. This may be related to weaker selective pressure in the heterozygous state, incomplete penetrance, partial functional redundancy among ATG4 family members, or their specific role in neuronal antiviral immunity.

It must be emphasized that this study focuses on biallelic mutations — that is, cases in which both alleles of a given gene carry distinct damaging variants, each inherited from one parent. This configuration is critical in autosomal recessive disorders, where both alleles must typically be affected to impair gene function. The rarity and predicted deleteriousness of these variants strongly support their potential functional impact, thereby further supporting the hypothesis that rare deleterious biallelic ATG4 gene variants are enriched in patients with severe central nervous system viral infections.

However, gnomAD data alone provide information on population frequency and variant constraint, but cannot independently establish the pathogenicity of these variants. We therefore performed in vitro expression studies of ATG4C and ATG4D variants to further verify whether these variants affect expression at the mRNA and protein levels.

## 3.2 In Vitro Expression of ATG4C and ATG4D Variants

### 3.2.1 Candidate Variant Selection

Based on the population genetic analysis described above, we aimed to systematically evaluate the effects of these variants on ATG4C and ATG4D transcription and expression in vitro. We established selection criteria of homozygous nonsynonymous variants with MAF > 0.0001 or AM score > 0.56, together with patient-derived biallelic nonsynonymous variants (Fig. 3.2). This strategy yielded 16 ATG4C variants and 10 ATG4D variants (Tables 3.1 and 3.2). Among these, ATG4C Gln216Ter, Arg389Ter, c.797-2A>C, Trp203Leufs*4, and Val320Glyfs*8 were pLOF variants.

### 3.2.2 Effects of ATG4C Variants on Transcription and Protein Expression

We assessed ATG4C mRNA expression levels in HEK293T cells expressing wild-type or variant ATG4C by RT-qPCR to determine whether these variants affect expression at the transcriptional level (Fig. 3.3A). Integrating results from two independent primer pairs targeting exons 2–3 and exons 7–8, most ATG4C variants did not lead to reduced mRNA levels. Arg389Ter, Trp203Leufs*4, Glu216Ter, Lys371Arg, Lys326Gln, Asp442Tyr, Asn75Ser, Thr33Met, Pro60Ser, Lys155Gln, His197Leu, Gln122Arg, and Arg80His showed mRNA expression levels comparable to or higher than wild-type. Several of these variants showed elevated levels relative to wild-type in both primer pairs, suggesting that they do not act by reducing ATG4C mRNA abundance.

Notably, Val320Glyfs*8 and c.797-2A>C exhibited marked regional detection differences: both showed signal higher than wild-type with the exon 2–3 primer pair, but signals near empty-vector levels with the exon 7–8 primer pair. This indicates that the upstream transcript region of these variants was intact without global mRNA degradation, but the normal exon 7–8 detection signal was essentially lost. We interpret this result as follows: because Val320Glyfs*8 and c.797-2A>C are located in or near the exon 6–7 region of ATG4C, they likely alter the local sequence or transcript structure within the exon 7–8 amplification region, thereby severely impairing primer–cDNA template binding and producing artifactually low expression signals in that detection system. In contrast, the exon 2–3 region, located upstream of these mutations, was not directly affected. Therefore, for Val320Glyfs*8 and c.797-2A>C, the exon 2–3 primer results more accurately reflect genuine ATG4C mRNA levels. Conversely, Tyr25Asp exhibited the opposite pattern of inconsistency: exon 2–3 signal was markedly lower than wild-type whereas exon 7–8 signal was near wild-type levels, suggesting that the exon 2–3 detection abnormality may be a local effect and does not support global ATG4C mRNA reduction.

(B) Western blot analysis of ATG4C protein expression levels in HEK293T cells expressing wild-type or variant ATG4C. (C) ATG4C variants affecting protein levels are all located within the catalytic domain.

After completing RT-qPCR analysis of ATG4C/D variant mRNA levels, we performed Western blotting to assess ATG4C protein expression, translation product size, and protein stability in HEK293T cells (Fig. 3.3B).

The results revealed marked differences among ATG4C variants at the protein level. We were unable to detect ATG4C protein for four predicted pLOF variants: Val320Glyfs*8, Trp203Leufs*4, Glu216Ter, and c.797-2A>C. Examination of the positions of these pLOF variants within the ATG4C protein structure showed that they are all located within or affect the catalytic domain region (Fig. 3.3C). Trp203Leufs*4 and Glu216Ter occur in the middle of the ATG4C protein, directly causing a frameshift or premature stop codon within the catalytic domain, truncating the protein before complete catalytic domain folding. Val320Glyfs*8, although positioned relatively downstream, is still within the catalytic domain-associated region; the frameshift alters the downstream amino acid sequence and rapidly introduces a premature stop codon, disrupting the C-terminal portion of the catalytic domain and its downstream C-terminal structure. The c.797-2A>C variant likely alters the reading frame through a deletion, leading to an aberrant truncated protein or erroneous translation product. Because the catalytic domain is essential for proper protein folding and stability, the premature termination, frameshift, or deletion caused by these variants likely disrupts the protein core structure, preventing stable conformation formation and leading to recognition and degradation by the cellular protein quality control system.

When combined with the RT-qPCR results, these ATG4C variants primarily affect protein translation, folding, or stability rather than transcription. The four pLOF variants — Val320Glyfs*8, Trp203Leufs*4, Glu216Ter, and c.797-2A>C — all likely impair ATG4C protein production and stability by disrupting the catalytic domain, causing protein truncation or aberrant conformation, ultimately leading to loss of ATG4C protein.

### 3.2.3 Effects of ATG4D Variants on Transcription and Protein Expression

We selected primers targeting ATG4D exons 2–3 and exons 6–7; all other experimental methods and parameter settings were identical to those used for ATG4C. RT-qPCR results are shown in Fig. 3.4A.

Unlike the ATG4C group, in which some variants showed marked regional detection differences, the ATG4D variants exhibited generally consistent trends across both primer pairs, with no observation of signal retention in one region while the other region approached empty-vector levels. Overall, all ATG4D variants showed mRNA expression levels clearly above empty-vector levels, indicating detectable ATG4D transcript expression. However, compared with wild-type, different variants showed varying degrees of mRNA reduction.

Arg382His, Ser89Asn, Tyr280Cys, and Ser420del maintained transcript abundance relatively close to wild-type levels across both probe sets, suggesting that these variants do not cause substantial ATG4D mRNA reduction. By contrast, Glu374Asp, Ala44Pro, and Asn314His showed mild reductions in both probe sets, suggesting these variants may modestly affect ATG4D mRNA abundance or stability. Leu416Phe, Phe128Leu, and Pro426Arg showed more pronounced mRNA reductions relative to wild-type, although their expression remained substantially above empty-vector levels.

Western blotting revealed that, unlike the protein loss observed for several ATG4C pLOF variants, all tested ATG4D variants (predominantly missense variants and a small in-frame deletion, Ser420del) expressed full-length protein with molecular weight consistent with wild-type (Fig. 3.4B). Based on the locations of the ATG4D variants (Fig. 3.4C), none of these variants caused obvious frameshift or premature termination, did not disrupt the overall translation process, and did not lead to rapid intracellular protein degradation. However, ATG4D Ser89Asn and Tyr280Cys have been reported to cause a neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by speech and motor impairment [19], suggesting that these ATG4D variants may not act through reducing protein stability or blocking translation.

(B) Western blot analysis of ATG4D protein expression levels in HEK293T cells expressing wild-type or variant ATG4D. (C) ATG4D variants affecting protein levels are located in the mitochondrial signal peptide and catalytic domain.

Integrating the RT-qPCR and Western blot results: although ATG4D variants differed in their effects on transcript levels (with some showing mild to more pronounced mRNA reductions), these transcriptional decreases did not translate into overt protein loss. In the transient expression system, all variants maintained stable full-length protein expression. Therefore, these ATG4D variants likely do not act by reducing protein stability or blocking translation; their potential functional consequences more probably involve effects on protein activity, substrate recognition, protein–protein interactions, or subcellular localization.
