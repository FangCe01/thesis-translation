# Abstract

Autophagy is a critical cellular process for maintaining homeostasis and defending against viral infection, and it may play a particularly important role in antiviral immunity within the central nervous system. This study aimed to investigate how variants in the autophagy-related genes *ATG4C* and *ATG4D* affect human antiviral immunity. We first analyzed the distribution, population frequency, and predicted deleteriousness of *ATG4C* and *ATG4D* variants using large-scale population genetic databases. We then selected variants that were either relatively common in the general population or predicted to be highly deleterious, overexpressed them in human cells, and assessed their effects on mRNA and protein expression. The results showed that nonsynonymous variants in *ATG4C* and *ATG4D* with high predicted deleteriousness were generally rare in the population, suggesting that both genes are under strong evolutionary constraint. In vitro expression experiments further revealed that some predicted loss-of-function (pLOF) variants in ATG4C caused loss of protein expression, whereas the tested ATG4D variants did not lead to overt protein loss, although they may still affect protein function through other mechanisms. Viral infection assays further suggested that certain deleterious ATG4C variants may increase cellular susceptibility to herpes simplex virus infection. Collectively, this study found that rare deleterious biallelic variants in *ATG4C* and *ATG4D* are enriched in patients with severe central nervous system viral infections, suggesting that rare biallelic deleterious variants in *ATG4* genes may represent a previously unrecognized class of autosomal recessive inborn errors of immunity.

**Keywords:** autophagy; ATG4 family; ATG4C; ATG4D; antiviral immunity; central nervous system viral infection; genetic variants; inborn errors of immunity
