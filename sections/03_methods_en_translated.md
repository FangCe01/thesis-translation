# 2 Materials and Methods

## 2.1 Population Genetic Analysis of ATG4C and ATG4D

### 2.1.1 Identification of ATG4C and ATG4D Variants

This study was based on genetic data from patients who had undergone whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing at the Human Genomics in Infectious Diseases Laboratory (HGID, directed by Dr. Jean-Laurent Casanova). We screened for patients carrying biallelic variants in ATG4 family genes — that is, patients in whom both alleles of a given gene carried distinct damaging variants, each inherited from one parent. This genetic configuration is critical in autosomal recessive disorders, where both alleles must typically be affected to impair gene function. The screening revealed that the ATG4 variants identified in patients were restricted to *ATG4C* and *ATG4D*. Therefore, subsequent analyses focused exclusively on these two genes.

To assess the tolerance of *ATG4C* and *ATG4D* to genetic variation from a population genetics perspective, we systematically retrieved variant information for both genes from the gnomAD v4.1 database (Genome Aggregation Database) [17]. gnomAD integrates large-scale population exome and genome sequencing data, providing allele frequencies, heterozygote counts, homozygote counts, and population-specific distributions for each variant, and is widely used to evaluate population tolerance and potential functional constraint of genetic variants.

We focused on nonsynonymous variants present in the homozygous state, with particular attention to predicted loss-of-function (pLOF) variants. The rationale for this strategy is that the presence of homozygous nonsynonymous variants in the general population generally suggests some degree of tolerance to that variant or to impairment of the corresponding gene function. Conversely, variants that severely affect gene function are typically subject to negative selection and rarely appear in the homozygous state. We prioritized pLOF variants — which include nonsense, frameshift, and essential splice-site variants — because they can lead to protein truncation, mRNA degradation, or complete loss of protein function, and are therefore considered to have high potential pathogenicity and serve as important indicators of population tolerance.

### 2.1.2 Variant Pathogenicity and Population Frequency Analysis

After identifying eligible variants, we analyzed the distribution characteristics of pLOF and missense variants in *ATG4C* and *ATG4D* using CADD (Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion) scores and MAF (Minor Allele Frequency) [15]. CADD scores predict the potential deleteriousness of variants, with higher scores indicating a greater likelihood of affecting gene or protein function. MAF reflects the frequency of a variant in the population, with lower frequencies generally indicating greater rarity. Finally, we compared the CADD score of each variant against the corresponding gene's MSC95 threshold (Mutation Significance Cutoff) to determine whether the variants exceeded gene-specific pathogenicity thresholds, thereby enabling population genetic analysis of *ATG4C* and *ATG4D* [16].

## 2.2 In Vitro Expression Analysis of ATG4C and ATG4D Variants

### 2.2.1 Selection of Candidate ATG4C and ATG4D Variants

To further validate the population tolerance and predicted deleteriousness of ATG4C and ATG4D variants, we expressed in a cell-based system both rare patient-associated variants and nonsynonymous ATG4C/ATG4D variants from gnomAD v4.1 that were either relatively common (MAF > 0.0001) or predicted to be highly pathogenic (AlphaMissense (AM) score > 0.56, as well as pLOF variants), and analyzed their CADD scores [18].

The filtering criterion MAF > 0.0001 was designed to select variants that are relatively common in the population. Such variants serve as a "natural population experiment" to assess the tolerance of ATG4C and ATG4D to genetic variation, helping to establish a background distribution of tolerable variants.

In addition, we used AlphaMissense, a computational model that predicts the functional impact of missense variants, to evaluate the potential effects of amino acid substitutions on protein function. Higher scores indicate a greater likelihood of deleteriousness. According to the AlphaMissense classification, AM scores > 0.56 generally correspond to a "likely pathogenic/likely deleterious" range. Adding this criterion therefore allowed us to capture candidate deleterious missense variants that, while rare in the population, may have high destructive potential at the protein functional level.

The combined filtering strategy of "MAF > 0.0001 or AM score > 0.56" captured two categories of interpretively valuable variants: (1) relatively common background variants that can be used to assess whether a biallelic state is tolerated in the population, and (2) rare variants predicted by computational models to be high-risk candidate deleterious variants. We then further evaluated the selected variants by comparing their CADD scores against the MSC95 threshold for each gene, to distinguish background variants likely to be tolerated in the population from damaging variants subject to negative selection with potential functional impact. This strategy enabled assessment of the degree of genetic constraint on biallelic ATG4C and ATG4D variants and provided a population genetic framework for interpreting the potential pathogenicity of rare, high-impact variants identified in patients with viral encephalitis.

### 2.2.2 Selection and Construction of the In Vitro Expression System

To functionally evaluate the selected ATG4C and ATG4D variants, we established an in vitro expression system to assess their effects on mRNA levels, protein expression, protein size, and protein stability. Wild-type and variant constructs were compared under identical cell background and plasmid backbone conditions, minimizing interference from endogenous gene expression, promoter activity, or genetic background differences.

We employed a transient transfection system in HEK293T cells as a rapid screening platform. This system allowed simultaneous testing of multiple variants and avoided biases potentially introduced during stable cell line construction, such as antibiotic selection, clonal expansion, genomic integration effects, and long-term cellular adaptive changes. The effects of variants were then assessed by RT-qPCR and Western blotting to determine whether they reduced transcript levels, affected mRNA stability or translation, produced truncated proteins, or decreased protein stability.

HEK293T cells were chosen for their ease of culture, rapid growth, and high plasmid transfection efficiency, providing ample RNA and protein samples for subsequent analyses. ATG4C/D wild-type and variant expression constructs were generated in the pCMV3 vector, which contains a strong CMV promoter for high-level expression in HEK293T cells, facilitating detection of mRNA and protein by RT-qPCR and Western blotting.

For ATG4C/D protein detection, we constructed N-terminal Myc-tagged, C-terminal Myc-tagged, and untagged constructs. The Myc tag is short in sequence and can be sensitively detected using anti-Myc antibodies. Because some variants — such as frameshift, nonsense, or deletion variants — may disrupt the C-terminal region of the protein, C-terminal tags in these cases may not be translated, potentially yielding false-negative results by Western blot. Therefore, subsequent experiments used N-terminally Myc-tagged ATG4C/D constructs. This design permitted stable detection of both full-length proteins and C-terminally truncated or deleted proteins.

Although the HEK293T transient transfection system was suitable for rapid screening of ATG4C/D variant expression, it has certain limitations. HEK293T cells are transformed cells whose innate immune or antiviral signaling pathways may be altered. Moreover, transient transfection typically produces high but heterogeneous exogenous gene expression within a short expression window. This model is therefore insufficient for evaluating the longer-term effects of ATG4C/D variants on viral infection and host antiviral defense. To address these limitations, a stable expression system in BJ1 cells may serve as a more appropriate model. BJ1 cells are non-tumorigenic human fibroblasts that more closely resemble normal somatic cells and retain more intact innate immune and stress responses. The lentiviral stable expression system can also achieve sustained and relatively homogeneous ATG4C/D expression, which is important for subsequent studies of HSV-1 infection, interferon responses, autophagic flux, and viral replication. With this in mind, I also constructed pTRIP-GFP lentiviral vectors to facilitate the subsequent establishment of stable BJ1 cell lines by our collaborator.

**RT-qPCR**

We used RT-qPCR to measure ATG4C wild-type and variant mRNA levels in HEK293T cells to determine whether these variants affect transcript abundance. Detection was performed using TaqMan probe-based chemistry. Primers and probes were designed spanning exon–exon junctions to enhance specificity for mature spliced mRNA and to minimize signal interference from residual genomic DNA.

Because ATG4C variants are distributed across different exons, we designed two independent detection systems targeting the exon 2–3 and exon 7–8 junctions, respectively. The exon 2–3 system provided readout of the upstream transcript region, whereas the exon 7–8 system offered a mid/downstream detection window. Assaying two independent regions enabled cross-validation of mRNA abundance. Consistent reduction in mRNA levels across both detection systems suggested decreased transcript levels or stability; changes detected by only one system may indicate local amplification bias, aberrant splicing, or region-specific transcript alterations. The same strategy was applied to ATG4D, using detection systems targeting the exon 2–3 and exon 6–7 junctions.

**pCMV3-ATG4C/D Expression Plasmid Construction**

Wild-type pCMV3-ATG4C and pCMV3-ATG4D plasmids were constructed by a collaborator. ATG4C/D variants were introduced by site-directed mutagenesis. Overlap-extension PCR was performed using Invitrogen Platinum™ SuperFi™ II PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog 12368010) to introduce the specified mutations into the target sequence. After PCR amplification, methylated template plasmids were digested with DpnI (New England Biolabs, catalog R0176V) to remove unmutated parental template.

Mutated plasmid products were transformed into competent cells (New England Biolabs 10-beta Competent *E. coli*, catalog C2019H). After transformation, the bacterial suspension was spread on LB agar plates containing 0.1% ampicillin or kanamycin for selection based on the resistance gene carried by the plasmid. Single colonies were picked the following day and inoculated into LB liquid medium containing 0.1% ampicillin for expansion culture. Plasmids were extracted using the QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit (Qiagen, catalog 27106). All constructed plasmids were verified by sequencing (Plasmidsaurus).

**pTRIP-GFP-ATG4C/D Expression Plasmid Construction**

To construct pTRIP-GFP-ATG4C and pTRIP-GFP-ATG4D plasmids, we obtained the empty pTRIP-GFP vector from a commercial source. Linearized vector backbone was generated by PCR using the pTRIP-GFP plasmid as template. After amplification, template plasmid was digested with DpnI (New England Biolabs, catalog R0176V) to remove residual circular parental plasmid, and the linearized vector backbone was purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, catalog 28106).

ATG4C or ATG4D target fragments were amplified by PCR using the previously constructed and sequence-verified pCMV3-GFP-ATG4C or pCMV3-GFP-ATG4D plasmids as templates. PCR reactions were performed using Invitrogen Platinum™ SuperFi II PCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog 12368010). Amplification products were verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis to confirm correct fragment size, and then purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen, catalog 28106).

The purified linearized pTRIP-GFP backbone and ATG4C/D inserts were ligated using In-Fusion cloning to generate pTRIP-GFP-ATG4C or pTRIP-GFP-ATG4D expression plasmids. Ligation products were transformed into 10-beta Competent *E. coli* (New England Biolabs, catalog C2019H). After transformation, the bacterial suspension was spread on LB agar plates containing 0.1% ampicillin for selection. Single colonies were picked the next day and inoculated into LB liquid medium containing 0.1% ampicillin for expansion culture. Plasmids were extracted using the QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit (Qiagen, catalog 27106). All final plasmids were verified by sequencing (Plasmidsaurus).

**Cell Culture**

HEK-293T cells (CRL-3216; ATCC) were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and cultured in DMEM medium (Gibco, catalog 10566-016) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cells were maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO₂, 95% air incubator. All cells were routinely tested to confirm the absence of mycoplasma contamination.

**Transient Transfection**

Transient transfection of HEK293T cells was performed using X-tremeGENE™ 9 DNA Transfection Reagent. Approximately 24 h before transfection, HEK293T cells were seeded in culture plates or flasks to reach 30–40% confluence at the time of transfection. On the day of transfection, X-tremeGENE™ 9 DNA Transfection Reagent, plasmid DNA, and Opti-MEM® reduced-serum medium (Gibco, catalog 31985-070) were equilibrated to 15–25°C and gently vortexed. Appropriate volumes of Opti-MEM® were added to a sterile centrifuge tube, followed by X-tremeGENE™ 9 DNA Transfection Reagent. After gentle mixing, plasmid DNA was added and the mixture was homogenized by gentle pipetting. The transfection mixture was incubated for 15 min at 15–25°C to allow DNA–transfection reagent complex formation. Following incubation, the transfection complexes were added dropwise to HEK293T cell culture wells or flasks, and the plate was gently swirled to distribute the complexes evenly throughout the culture medium. Cells were returned to standard culture conditions and harvested 48 h post-transfection for subsequent RNA extraction, protein extraction, and expression analyses.

**Real-Time Quantitative Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR)**

RNA was isolated from HEK293T cells using the Quick-RNA MicroPrep Kit with Zymo-Spin IC columns (Zymo Research, catalog R1051) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Expression of ATG4C (spanning exons 2–3; spanning exons 7–8), ATG4D (spanning exons 2–3; spanning exons 6–7), and the reference gene β-glucuronidase (GUS, #4310888E) was detected using Applied Biosystems TaqMan probes on an Applied Biosystems 7500 Fast real-time PCR system. Results were analyzed using Applied Biosystems 7500 software (v.2.0.6) and expression was calculated using the ΔΔCt method according to the kit manufacturer's instructions.

**Western Blotting**

HEK293T cells were lysed in NP-40 lysis buffer (280 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris pH 8, 0.2 mM EDTA, 2 mM EGTA, 10% glycerol, 0.5% NP-40) supplemented with 1 mM DTT, PhosSTOP (Roche), and cOmplete protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). Protein lysates were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, which were then probed with unlabeled primary antibodies followed by secondary antibodies. Anti-Myc antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 2040; 1:1000 dilution) and GAPDH antibody (sc-47724, Santa Cruz Biotechnology; 1:5000 dilution) were obtained from commercial suppliers. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. HRP activity was visualized using SuperSignal West Pico chemiluminescent substrate (Thermo Fisher) and signals were detected with an Amersham Imager 600 (GE Life Sciences).

**Statistical Analysis**

Where applicable, results are presented as mean ± SEM. When required, log-transformed relative values were analyzed using linear mixed models to account for repeated measures. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (v.9.2.0).
