# 5 Discussion

My collaborator completed the construction of BJ1 cell lines stably expressing wild-type or variant ATG4C and performed experiments using an HSV-1/tdTomato infection model to assess the effects of different ATG4C variants on cellular susceptibility to HSV-1 infection. The experimental results (Fig. 5.1) showed that, with the empty-vector (EV) group set to 100% HSV-1/tdTomato-positive cells, the wild-type group had a positive rate approximately 50% of the EV group. Using the WT and EV groups as references, the viral positivity rates for ATG4C pLOF variants — including Arg389Ter, Val320Glyfs*8, Trp203Leufs*4, Glu216Ter, and c.797-2A>C — were all close to EV group levels. This result is consistent with the preceding Western blot data showing that Val320Glyfs*8, Trp203Leufs*4, Glu216Ter, and c.797-2A>C lead to loss of ATG4C protein, confirming that these predicted pLOF variants are indeed loss-of-function variants that render cells more susceptible to HSV-1 infection. These findings further support the core hypothesis of this study: that rare biallelic deleterious ATG4 gene variants are enriched in patients with severe central nervous system viral infections.

**Fig. 5.1 Effects of ATG4C variants on HSV-1 susceptibility in BJ1 stable cell lines.** BJ1 cell lines stably expressing wild-type or variant ATG4C were established by lentiviral transduction, and the effects of different ATG4C variants on cellular susceptibility to HSV-1 infection were assessed by HSV-1 infection experiments.

UniProt annotation indicates that the catalytic activity of ATG4C depends on a Cys-His-Asp catalytic triad composed of Cys11, Asp345, and His347. Cys11 serves as the nucleophilic cysteine residue central to the cysteine protease catalytic reaction; Asp345 and His347 together with Cys11 constitute the catalytic center involved in the cleavage and delipidation of ATG8-family proteins. Therefore, any variant that results in loss of catalytic residues, aberrant protein spatial conformation, or impaired catalytic center stability may compromise ATG4C-mediated autophagy function and affect the ability of cells to restrict HSV-1 infection.

In this study, Western blot results showed that pLOF variants — Val320Glyfs*8, Trp203Leufs*4, Glu216Ter, and c.797-2A>C — exhibited loss of the corresponding protein, consistent with their HSV-1/tdTomato infection results showing viral positivity rates near EV group levels, supporting that these predicted pLOF variants are indeed loss-of-function variants. Although Arg389Ter is located downstream of Asp345 and His347 and theoretically preserves the catalytic triad, it leads to C-terminal truncation that may still affect protein stability, substrate recognition, or autophagy-related interactions, and thus may also cause a marked reduction in ATG4C antiviral function.

Beyond pLOF variants, several missense variants — including Pro60Ser, Val71Ile, Lys155Gln, and His197Leu — also exhibited varying degrees of functional impairment. These residues are not directly located at Cys11, Asp345, or His347, but may affect ATG4C function by altering local physicochemical properties, conformational stability, or protein surface interactions. Pro60Ser involves a change from the rigid proline to the polar serine, which may alter local backbone conformation and flexibility; its location in the N-terminal region may indirectly affect structural stability near Cys11. Val71Ile represents a conservative substitution between hydrophobic amino acids with minimal charge effects, but altered side-chain volume and spatial orientation may still influence local hydrophobic packing or conformational dynamics. Lys155Gln results in a change from positively charged lysine to uncharged polar glutamine, potentially causing local loss of positive charge that may affect salt bridges, surface electrostatic distribution, or substrate binding. His197Leu involves a change from histidine — which can participate in hydrogen bonding and weak electrostatic interactions — to hydrophobic leucine, which may disrupt local hydrogen-bond networks and increase hydrophobicity, thereby more readily affecting protein folding or conformational stability.

Overall, ATG4C variants can be classified into two categories: pLOF variants resulting from truncation, frameshift, or splicing abnormalities that cause severe loss of function primarily through protein loss, catalytic triad disruption, or C-terminal structural damage; and missense variants that, although not directly disrupting the catalytic triad, may lead to partial functional defects through changes in amino acid charge, polarity, hydrophobicity, and local conformation. In the HSV-1/tdTomato infection experiments, wild-type ATG4C significantly reduced the proportion of virus-positive cells, whereas pLOF variants and some missense variants failed to exert full protective effects, indicating that the intact ATG4C structure and normal protease activity are important for restricting HSV-1 infection. These results further support the involvement of ATG4C-mediated autophagy in anti-HSV-1 innate immunity and provide functional evidence for the pathogenic role of rare biallelic deleterious ATG4C variants in patients with severe central nervous system viral infections.

A growing body of research suggests that ATG4 family variants may contribute to multiple human neurological diseases through diverse pathogenic mechanisms. In addition to the aforementioned autophagy defects caused by rare deleterious variants in ATG4A and LC3B2 conferring susceptibility to HSV-2 infection in the human central nervous system, biallelic ATG4D variants have been found to cause a syndromic neurodevelopmental disorder primarily characterized by speech and motor impairment. Variants including Tyr280Cys, Asp356Asn, and Asp437Alafs*37 reduce ATG4D processing activity toward its primary substrate GABARAPL1 [19]. Interestingly, overall autophagosome formation and general autophagic flux in patient cells did not show obvious abnormalities, suggesting that ATG4D-related disease mechanisms may not involve simple global autophagy blockade, but rather specific substrate processing defects, tissue-specific functions, non-canonical autophagy, or autophagy-independent functional abnormalities. Animal model studies also support an important role for ATG4D in the nervous system: ATG4D-deficient mice exhibit cerebellar neurodegenerative changes [20], and a canine model carrying ATG4D missense variants displays progressive cerebellar ataxia and Purkinje cell loss [21]. ATG4 family variants may also participate in tumor-associated biological processes through effects on autophagic activity [22]. These findings indicate that the disease effects of ATG4 family variants are diverse, and their pathogenic mechanisms may extend beyond canonical autophagy defects to include substrate-specific processing, tissue-specific functions, non-canonical autophagy, and autophagy-independent functional abnormalities.

Unfortunately, due to various limitations, this study has not yet systematically elucidated the specific mechanisms by which these variants confer susceptibility to HSV-1 infection. Future studies should further examine key indicators including autophagic flux, LC3/GABARAP lipidation, p62 degradation, autophagosome formation, and autophagosome–lysosome fusion to determine whether the impaired HSV-1 control caused by ATG4C pLOF variants is entirely dependent on the canonical macroautophagy pathway or also involves non-canonical autophagy and autophagy-independent functions.

An increasing number of studies suggest that ATG4 homologs are not simply redundant but differ in substrate preference, tissue expression, subcellular localization, and non-canonical autophagy functions [23]. Therefore, future studies should conduct complementation experiments with wild-type and variant constructs in ATG4 single-knockout, multi-knockout, or quadruple-knockout backgrounds to more accurately assess the functional impact of different ATG4 variants. Moreover, the present study lacks validation in patient-derived cells, neuronal models, or animal models; future studies should incorporate primary fibroblasts, iPSC-derived neurons, and additional viral infection models to further clarify the role of ATG4C/D variants in central nervous system antiviral immunity.

From a translational perspective, this study provides new directions for genetic risk assessment, mechanistic classification, and intervention strategy exploration for severe central nervous system viral infections. ATG4C, ATG4D, and other ATG4 family genes may serve as candidate genes for genetic screening in patients with suspected viral encephalitis, recurrent herpesvirus infection, or unexplained severe central nervous system infections. For individuals carrying biallelic pLOF variants or functionally impaired missense variants, combination with family segregation analysis and in vitro functional validation may improve the diagnostic yield for rare genetic susceptibility factors and provide a basis for genetic counseling. Second, ATG4C functional status may serve as a biomarker for host antiviral autophagy capacity, helping to identify individuals at higher risk of severe disease or central nervous system dissemination following HSV-1 infection. The future establishment of a standardized ATG4C variant functional assessment platform — such as a high-throughput detection system based on LC3/GABARAP processing, autophagic flux, and viral replication inhibition — will facilitate rapid pathogenicity determination for rare variants identified in clinical settings.

Furthermore, this study also suggests that enhancing autophagy function may represent an adjunctive therapeutic strategy for some ATG4-associated virus-susceptible patients. Future studies could explore whether mTOR inhibitors, AMPK activators, lysosome function enhancers, or other small-molecule autophagy regulators can partially restore the ability of ATG4C/D functionally deficient cells to control HSV-1. For complete-loss pLOF variants, autophagy induction alone may be insufficient to correct the defect, and gene supplementation, mRNA delivery, or gene editing strategies may be more specifically targeted. ATG4C/D variant models may also be used for screening antiviral drugs and host-directed therapies, helping to identify candidate interventions that bypass ATG4C deficiency, enhance intracellular viral clearance, or improve neuronal antiviral capacity. With the establishment of patient-derived cells, iPSC neurons, and animal models, ATG4C-related research is expected to translate from genetic discovery to risk prediction, precision diagnosis, and individualized therapeutic strategies.
