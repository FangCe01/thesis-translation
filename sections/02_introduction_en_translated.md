# 1 Introduction

## 1.1 Background

### 1.1.1 Autophagy defects mediated by ATG4A and LC3B2 in patients with HSV-2 meningitis

Two adult patients with recurrent lymphocytic meningoencephalitis caused by herpes simplex virus type 2 (HSV-2) were recently reported to each carry rare monoallelic variants in the autophagy proteins ATG4A and LC3B2 [1]. Cells from patients carrying predicted deleterious variants in *ATG4A* and *MAP1LC3B2* exhibited impaired autophagy induction upon HSV-2 infection, accompanied by increased viral load and exacerbated cell death. That study provided the first evidence that autophagy serves an important antiviral function in human herpesvirus infection, and suggested that inborn errors of autophagy may represent a novel class of primary immunodeficiency. This finding implies that defects in ATG4A and its homologs — ATG4B, ATG4C, and ATG4D — may contribute to virus-associated tissue damage and disease progression.

### 1.1.2 The role of ATG4-mediated autophagy in antiviral immunity

Autophagy is a fundamental and highly conserved cellular process that removes or recycles damaged cellular components to maintain homeostasis [2,3]. This tightly regulated cascade, involving over 30 autophagy-related proteins (ATGs), is activated under various environmental stresses such as nutrient starvation, infection, inflammation, and oxidative stress [3–5]. Autophagy proceeds through the formation of double-membrane autophagosomes that engulf portions of the cytoplasm and specific intracellular substrates — including damaged macromolecules or organelles, intracellular pathogens, and protein aggregates that accumulate during oxidative stress, infection, disease, and aging [6]. The autophagosome then fuses with lysosomes to form autolysosomes, in which the contents are degraded and the resulting macromolecules are recycled [2].

Beyond its classical role in cellular homeostasis, a growing body of evidence indicates that autophagy is also an important component of the host response to a variety of intracellular pathogens, including viruses [7–10]. Notably, autophagy-mediated antiviral immunity may be particularly critical in the central nervous system. Neurons are terminally differentiated, long-lived cells with limited capacity for renewal or replacement; they must therefore eliminate viral threats while preserving cell survival and function. The type I interferon response, which constitutes the principal innate immune mechanism in mitotic cells, often triggers systemic reactions [11] that can cause neuronal damage. Autophagy may therefore serve as a local, cell-intrinsic form of innate immunity. This mechanism is especially crucial for neurons, enabling them to mount an antiviral response while maintaining the integrity and functionality of the nervous system.

Among all autophagy proteins, ATG4 is the sole cysteine protease and a key regulator of the autophagy pathway [12]. In humans, the ATG4 family comprises four homologs — ATG4A, ATG4B, ATG4C, and ATG4D — which collectively mediate the processing and delipidation of human ATG8-family proteins (e.g., LC3 and GABARAP subfamilies). Although these homologs share structural similarity, they exhibit marked differences in substrate preference, regulatory properties, and tissue-specific expression patterns [13,14]. Through these functions, the ATG4 family orchestrates autophagosome initiation and maturation and is indispensable for maintaining appropriate autophagic flux and cellular homeostasis.

## 1.2 Research Scope

Building on the specialized role of autophagy in neuronal antiviral immunity and the central position of the ATG4 family in autophagy regulation, this study aimed to evaluate the population tolerance of *ATG4C* and *ATG4D* variants and to experimentally validate their predicted deleteriousness. To this end, we performed population genetic analyses of *ATG4C* and *ATG4D* using the gnomAD v4.1 database and the HGID laboratory database, assessing variant pathogenicity and evolutionary constraint through minor allele frequency (MAF), Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) scores, and the Mutation Significance Cutoff (MSC95) threshold [15,16]. On this basis, we further selected nonsynonymous homozygous variants from gnomAD v4.1 that were either relatively common in the population or predicted to be highly deleterious, constructed wild-type and variant expression plasmids for ATG4C and ATG4D, and assessed their effects on transcription and protein expression in HEK293T cells, comparing them with patient-derived ATG4C/D variants. Our experimental results showed that ATG4C/D variants are subject to strong selective pressure, and that rare deleterious biallelic variants are enriched in patients with severe central nervous system viral infections. These findings suggest that ATG4C/D-related inborn autophagy defects may increase susceptibility to CNS viral infections and may represent a class of as-yet inadequately recognized primary immunodeficiencies. This study provides a new perspective on the role of ATG4-mediated autophagy in human antiviral immunity.
